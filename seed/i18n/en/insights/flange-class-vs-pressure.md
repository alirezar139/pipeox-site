One of the most common misconceptions in flange selection is to treat the class number as the working pressure — to assume that a **Class 150** flange withstands 150 psi. In fact, the pressure class is a "rating", and the actual allowable pressure depends on **temperature** and **material**.

## What is a pressure class?

In **ASME B16.5** (for flanges up to 24 inches), flanges are grouped into Classes 150, 300, 600, 900, 1500 and 2500. For each class and each **material group**, a table of allowable pressures at different temperatures (the pressure–temperature rating) is given.

## The role of temperature

As temperature rises, the strength of steel drops and so does the allowable pressure. For example, for an A105 carbon-steel flange (material group 1.1):

| Class | Approx. allowable pressure at ambient temperature | At about 200 °C |
|---|---|---|
| 150 | about 19.6 bar | about 13.8 bar |
| 300 | about 51 bar | about 43.8 bar |
| 600 | about 102 bar | about 87.6 bar |

*These figures are approximate and only illustrate the concept; for design, always refer to the current edition of the ASME B16.5 tables.*

## The role of material

Flanges of the same class in different materials — for example A105, A350 LF2 or A182 F316 — fall into different material groups and have their own pressure–temperature tables. That is why changing the material without re-checking the class can put the safety of the line at risk.

## How to choose the right class

1. Take the **design pressure and temperature** of the line from the process data.
2. Determine the flange's **material group**.
3. In the ASME B16.5 tables, find the lowest class whose allowable pressure at the design temperature exceeds the design pressure.
4. Check consistency with the **class of the connected valves and equipment**.
5. Select the **gasket** and **bolting** for the same class and service.

## The facing matters too

Raised face (RF) is the most common option. In higher classes and critical services, ring-type joint (RTJ) facing with a metal gasket is used. Flat face (FF) is usually used for connections to cast-iron or low-pressure equipment.
