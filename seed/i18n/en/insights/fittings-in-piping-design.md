Fittings are a small share of a pipeline's weight, but they directly affect pressure drop, installation space, line stresses and even piggability. Choosing them well starts at the design stage.

## Elbows: long or short radius?

- **Long radius (LR):** bend radius of 1.5 × the nominal diameter. Lower pressure drop and smoother flow; the default choice for most lines.
- **Short radius (SR):** bend radius equal to the nominal diameter. For tight spaces, but with higher pressure drop and erosion.
- **Longer bends (3D and 5D):** on transmission lines that must be **pigged**, large-radius bends are used instead of standard elbows.

## Tees: equal or reducing?

An **equal** tee is used when the branch has the same diameter as the main line. For a smaller branch, a **reducing** tee is more economical than an equal tee plus a separate reducer, and it saves one weld. For branches much smaller than the main line, forged branch fittings (such as Weldolet and Sockolet) are the usual choice.

## Reducers: concentric or eccentric?

- **Concentric:** both ends share the same axis; common on vertical lines and pump discharges.
- **Eccentric:** one side stays flat. At a **pump suction** it is installed "flat side up" so that air does not collect and the risk of cavitation is reduced; on pipe racks it is installed "flat side down" to keep the bottom of the pipe level.

## Matching the pipe

A butt-weld fitting must match the pipe it is welded to:

- **Material:** for example A234 WPB for A106 Gr.B pipe.
- **Wall thickness:** the same schedule (SCH) as the pipe, so that welding and inspection are done correctly.
- **Dimensional standard:** ASME B16.9 for butt-weld fittings, and ASME B16.11 for forged socket-weld and threaded fittings.

---

When requesting a quote for fittings, state the **type, angle or radius, size (and the second size for reducers), wall schedule, material and standard** for each item; a simple MTO table is the best format.
